Nvidia Sold RTX 50 Founders Edition Cards at MSRP at PAX West, and the 5090 Beat Retail by $2,200

September 7, 2026September 7, 2026 crmaris

List prices still exist. You just have to fly to Seattle and find one man on the show floor.

Nvidia spent the weekend selling RTX 50 Founders Edition cards at their original list prices at PAX West, and the gap between those numbers and what the same cards cost in a shop has stopped being funny. An RTX 5090 went for $1,999, an RTX 5080 for $999, an RTX 5070 for $549. Micro Center, one of the cheaper places to actually find a 5090 in stock, has been asking somewhere around $4,200. Plenty of listings sit north of $5,000.

There was no booth queue and no timed online drop. Jacob Freeman, Nvidia’s GeForce Evangelist, worked the floor handing out purchase codes and posted his location on X as he moved, with most attendees ending up pointed toward the convention centre’s Skybridge. The codes get redeemed on Nvidia’s own web store, so nobody walked out of PAX carrying a graphics card, but the price on the other end of the code is the real one. Tom’s Hardware and TechPowerUp both logged the pricing.

The flight is cheaper than the card

PC Gamer did the arithmetic that makes this worth writing about. A short-notice flight from the UK to Seattle came to roughly GBP 1,000, call it $1,352. The priciest PAX day pass is $89. Add the $1,999 card and the whole trip lands at $3,440, several hundred dollars under what a 5090 costs from a retailer that has one on the shelf. When the spread between a product’s list price and its street price is wider than the list price itself, the list price has stopped describing anything.

This is not a new format for Nvidia. Verified Priority Access started in 2022 as a lottery for RTX 40 Founders Editions and returned for the RTX 50 generation, and while the online signup still exists, the company has steadily shifted MSRP allocation toward live events, the 2025 food-truck stunt being the most-mocked example. What has changed is the size of the prize. In 2022 winning the lottery meant skipping a queue. In 2026 it means a couple of thousand dollars.

Memory is the reason, and it is not easing

None of this is scalping in the 2021 sense. The pressure is coming from memory. A 64GB DDR5-5600 kit averaged around $1,118 in August against roughly $191 a year earlier, and AI datacentre buying has done much the same to the GDDR and HBM supply that graphics cards draw from. AMD is reported to be raising graphics card prices by at least 10% this year for exactly that reason. A 5090 carries 32GB of GDDR7, and the bill of materials underneath that number has moved a long way since launch.

Which is also the honest caveat. Handing out codes at a convention does not touch the cost structure, and Nvidia has not said how many cards were available, how the allocation split across models, or whether it plans to keep doing this. It is a goodwill gesture with a very small denominator, geographically gated to whoever could reach Seattle between 4 and 7 September. Read it as evidence of how far retail has drifted, not as a fix.

The more useful takeaway for anyone shopping right now is that $1,999 is still what Nvidia thinks a 5090 is worth. Everything above it is the market, and the market is being set by buyers purchasing memory in quantities no gamer competes with. VideoCardz notes the offer ran only for the duration of the show.
