Jet stream (EN)

Jet streams are fast-flowing, narrow air currents in the upper troposphere (~9–16 km altitude) that travel from west to east at speeds of 100–400 km/h. Their position and strength have a direct impact on surface weather: a southward dip brings cold Arctic air into mid-latitudes, while a northward bulge allows warm subtropical air to surge poleward.

There are two main jet streams per hemisphere: the stronger polar jet (9–12 km) and the weaker subtropical jet (10–16 km). The polar jet is the one most relevant to European and Italian weather, especially in autumn and winter when it intensifies.

Jet streams form because of two combined effects:

  • Large-scale atmospheric circulation cells (Polar, Ferrel, Hadley) driven by differential solar heating
  • The Coriolis force from Earth's rotation deflecting those air masses eastward
